# Why $80 U.S.-Made Jeans Don't Work: The Production Costing Reality Most Designers Never See

**By Kerri Bridgman**

---

"I want to sell a pair of jeans for $80 retail and have them made in the United States."

It is one of the most common statements I hear from fashion founders. And it is one of the clearest examples of the gap between how designers think about pricing and how production actually works.

The intention behind it is sound. A founder wants to offer quality denim at an accessible price point, made domestically, with integrity. There is nothing wrong with that vision.

But when you run the real numbers, the math tells a different story. And that story is the reason so many fashion brands struggle with pricing — not because they lack taste or ambition, but because they have never been shown the actual cost structure of production.

## The Assumption That Starts the Problem

When most founders estimate production cost, they think in three categories:

**Fabric:** $12. **Sewing:** $18. **Hardware:** $3.

That puts the estimated cost at $33 per unit. At an $80 retail price, the margin looks reasonable. Comfortable, even. Enough room to run the business.

But this model is missing most of the real costs required for sustainable production. It accounts for the factory layer and almost nothing else.

## What Production Actually Costs

Denim is significantly more complex than basic garments. It requires specialized handling, multiple layers of production, and a supply chain that most simple costing models do not account for.

A realistic U.S.-made denim cost breakdown looks more like this:

**Materials:**
- Premium denim fabric — $18.00
- Pocketing fabric — $2.00
- Hardware (rivets, zipper) — $4.00
- Patch and labels — $3.00

**Production and logistics:**
- Cut and sew labor — $30.00–$40.00
- Washing and finishing — $8.00
- Packaging and freight — $7.00

When you add it together — materials at $27, labor averaging $35, and operations at $15 — the true cost per unit lands between $72 and $82.

For a pair of jeans with an $80 retail price, that leaves a gross margin of roughly $8. Before marketing. Before professional photography. Before e-commerce fees, payment processing at 3–4%, returns, damaged goods, and unsold inventory.

The business loses money on every sale.

## The Margin Trap

This is the margin trap, and it is remarkably common. Founders set a retail price based on what feels right for their market or their brand positioning. They estimate production cost using the numbers they can see — fabric, sewing, hardware. The gap between the two looks like margin.

But it is not margin. It is an information gap.

The expenses that sit between the factory invoice and the actual cost of bringing a product to market are substantial: marketing spend, professional photoshoots, website and e-commerce platform fees, payment processing, return handling, and the cost of inventory that does not sell. These are not optional business expenses. They are the cost of operating a fashion brand.

When the production cost alone consumes 90% or more of the retail price, there is no room for the business to function. The founder works harder, the brand grows revenue, and profit never materializes.

## How the Industry Actually Prices

The fashion industry uses a markup structure that has been refined over decades, and it exists for a reason. It is the mathematical foundation of a sustainable business model.

The standard framework:

**Factory cost × 2.2–2.5 = wholesale price.**
**Wholesale price × 2.0 = retail price.**

In practice, that means a pair of jeans with a $65 factory cost would wholesale at approximately $145 and retail at $290.

This is why premium U.S.-made denim often sells for $200–$300. It is not because brands are overcharging. It is because they are pricing according to the real cost structure of domestic production.

When founders see these retail prices and assume the margins are excessive, they are usually looking at the wrong variable. The price is not inflated. The production cost is simply higher than most people realize.

## Designing Within Constraints

The insight that changes everything is this: the goal is not to force the price lower. The goal is to understand the system.

Once you have accurate visibility into your true cost structure — every material, every labor component, every logistics expense — pricing stops feeling like guesswork and starts feeling like strategy. You can design within the constraints of your business model rather than discovering those constraints after production is already committed.

There are five strategic levers that become available once you understand your numbers clearly.

**Materials.** Adjust fabric choice and hardware specifications. Moving from a premium selvedge denim to a quality non-selvedge option, or simplifying hardware selections, can shift the cost meaningfully without compromising the integrity of the product.

**Finishing.** Change wash treatments and distressing levels. Washing and finishing is one of the most variable cost categories in denim production. A raw or rinse finish costs significantly less than an enzyme wash with hand-distressing. The design decision and the cost decision are the same decision.

**Construction.** Simplify design details to reduce labor hours. Every additional stitch line, every extra pocket detail, every decorative element adds labor time. This is not about making a lesser product. It is about being intentional with where complexity lives in your design.

**Sourcing.** Move production locations to optimize for cost or speed. U.S. manufacturing offers advantages in speed, communication, and proximity. But if the price point cannot support domestic production, the strategic response is not to squeeze the margin — it is to evaluate whether offshore production aligns better with the business model.

**Pricing.** Raise the retail price to reflect true value and margin. This is the lever most founders resist, and it is often the most important one. If the product is well-made, if the materials are premium, if the brand has a clear point of view — the market will support a price that reflects those things. Underpricing does not make a brand more accessible. It makes it unsustainable.

## Aligning Retail Goals with Production Reality

One of the most useful frameworks in production costing is the alignment between your target retail price and the production cost it can actually support.

Using standard industry markups:

- **$80 retail** supports a production cost of roughly $16–$20
- **$150 retail** supports a production cost of roughly $30–$35
- **$250 retail** supports a production cost of roughly $50–$65

If your true production cost is $65–$82 per unit for U.S.-made denim, the retail price that supports that cost — with room for wholesale distribution, marketing, and operations — is not $80. It is $250 or higher.

This is not discouraging news. It is clarifying information. It tells you exactly what kind of product, what kind of sourcing, and what kind of positioning your business model requires.

## The Root Cause of Pricing Struggles

Designers do not struggle with pricing because they lack vision. They struggle because they have never been shown the real cost structure of fashion production.

Most design programs do not teach production costing at this level of detail. Most business resources for fashion founders focus on branding, marketing, or fundraising — not the operational math that determines whether a collection can be profitable before a single unit is sold.

The result is that founders enter production with conviction about their product and almost no framework for evaluating whether the economics of that product actually work. They set prices based on competitor comparison, gut instinct, or what their audience "will pay" — without first calculating what the product needs to cost to sustain the business behind it.

This is not a creative failure. It is an information gap.

And once that gap is closed — once a founder can look at a costing sheet and understand not just what each line item costs, but what the total cost structure means for their pricing, their margins, and their growth — the entire business shifts. Decisions become clearer. Pricing feels grounded instead of anxious. And production becomes something the founder controls rather than something that controls them.
